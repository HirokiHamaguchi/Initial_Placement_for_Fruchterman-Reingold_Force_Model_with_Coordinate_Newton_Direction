\reviewercomment{
  It should be noted that the experimental results in Section 5 show that the proposed initial layout algorithm works better when it is combined with the L-BFGS algorithm for computing final layouts.

\begin{response}
  First, we recognize that the previous version of the manuscript contained several expressions that could have caused misunderstanding, and we apologize for this.

  As a premise, Reviewer A's comment mainly concerns the comparison between algorithms used after initialization, namely the FR algorithm versus L-BFGS. However, what we truly intend to claim is a comparison of initialization methods: Random initialization versus Coordinate Newton initialization, or SA-based initialization versus Coordinate Newton initialization. Therefore, the observation that ``the proposed initial layout algorithm works better when it is combined with the L-BFGS algorithm for computing final layouts'' is attributable to the difference between the non-proposed components, and is not directly related to the superiority or inferiority of the proposed method itself.

  We considered carefully how best to respond to this point, and revised the manuscript in two directions.

  First, as stated above, we made it clear that the point raised concerns only the difference between existing post-initialization methods and is independent of the merit of the proposed method. It is true that the result labeled CN-LBFGS gives the best performance and is better than CN-FR. However, this reflects the difference between existing optimization methods, which is not the central focus of this paper. What we want readers to compare is the difference between RAND-FR and CN-FR, or between RAND-LBFGS and CN-LBFGS. We clarified in Section 5 that this is the comparison on which attention should be focused.

  Second, we revised expressions that could have caused the misunderstanding above. In the previous manuscript, as Reviewer B also noted, we referred to the superiority of L-BFGS in the introduction and elsewhere, which may have given the impression that L-BFGS itself was part of the proposed method. However, as illustrated for example in Fig.~1, the proposed method and L-BFGS are completely independent components. If a reader wishes to use the FR algorithm, they may do so; indeed, using the proposed method in combination with FR is also within the intended scope of the method. We revised the wording in the introduction and related parts so that this distinction is communicated more clearly.

  The discussion continues in the next response.
\end{response}

\subsection{Difference Between FR and KK Models}

\reviewercomment{
  Second, the authors do not sufficiently consider differences between the FR force model and others, especially the KK model, which becomes apparent especially in Subsection 7.1.

  Here the authors first say "Although we utilized the coordinate Newton direction only for the optimization problem (1), we can see that its application is not necessarily limited to the FR force model alone", and then present "objective functions arising from graphs".
  Does the authors mean that this discussion applies to other force models including the KK model?
  If so, I think that the authors should adopt the KK model instead of the FR model.
}

\begin{response}
  Thank you for this insightful comment. Our response to this point has two parts.

  First, as the reviewer suggests, the proposed method is applicable to other force models such as KK, HC, and Eades. This is because the coordinate Newton direction can be defined in the same way for functions of this form.
  Second, however, for the KK model, the effect of the proposed method may be less pronounced than for other models, or it may even be counterproductive, because of the specific characteristics of the KK model.

  We first discuss the generalization to the HC and Eades layouts. These force models are also mentioned in Hosobe [19], and the proposed method is indeed applicable to them. We also conducted numerical experiments. As described in detail in the revised manuscript, some additional care is required because of differences such as the presence or absence of convexity, but the proposed method was found to be effective in essentially the same way. We added explanations and numerical results on these points to the main text and the Appendix. Since these models are somewhat less widely known than the FR model, we refer to them only briefly in the introduction. We also considered changing the title of the paper, but decided to keep it unchanged.

  In contrast, for the KK model, we believe that the effect of the proposed method may be less apparent than for other models. This is closely related to whether the sparsity of the graph can be exploited. The proposed method is designed to compute the force on each vertex by looking at the vertices adjacent to it and to optimize the layout based on this computation. Therefore, when the main terms in the energy function are at most \(\order*{\abs{E}}\), that is, proportional to the number of edges, the proposed method can work much faster than naive methods requiring \(\order*{\abs{V}^2}\) computations, and its effect is likely to be significant. This is a common feature of the FR, HC, and Eades models. Indeed, the examples of ``objective functions arising from graphs'' also have this form.

  In the KK model, however, the main energy terms are defined for all pairs of vertices, rather than as a separate set of \(\order*{\abs{E}}\) terms. This is because the energy in the KK model is defined by first computing all-pairs graph distances and then defining energy terms based on those distances. Therefore, computing forces on a vertex-by-vertex basis does not provide the same advantage and may even cause slower convergence.

  As related work, a method based on another optimization approach called SGD has been proposed for the KK model: [33] J. X. Zheng, S. Pawar, and D. F. M. Goodman, ``Graph Drawing by Stochastic Gradient Descent,'' IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics. In contrast to the proposed method, that approach focuses on pairs rather than vertices when performing optimization, and in that sense it can be regarded as complementary to our work.

  Based on these considerations, we removed the previous Section 7.1 and instead revised the manuscript, especially around Section 4.5, to explain these issues more carefully.
\end{response}

\subsection{Effect of Initial Placement Over Time}

\reviewercomment{
  One thing that comes out of the charts in Figure 6 is that this different initial positioning has limited effect on the way the objective function decreases over time - which is an interesting phenomenon.
  In FR, the chart appears shifted below by how ``better'' the initial positioning is, with the non-CN version never catching up.
}

\begin{response}
  We believe that this behavior is caused by the slow convergence of the FR algorithm, i.e., the simulation-based algorithm. For the graphs used in Figure 6, there appears to be a nearly unique equilibrium state, and the methods appear to converge toward the same best objective value for each graph. Of course, the initial placement and the subsequent algorithm affect the path through which the objective function decreases. In other words, if sufficiently many iterations are allowed, all methods seem to converge toward the same visually clean graph layout.

  Therefore, we believe that it is somewhat inaccurate to say that the difference due to the initial placement remains permanently in the simulation-based algorithm. If a very large number of iterations is allowed, then even for the simulation-based algorithm, as for L-BFGS, the difference due to initialization gradually fades and the methods eventually converge to the same objective value. However, because the simulation-based algorithm converges slowly, a very large number of iterations would be required, and we stopped the plots at the current range because showing such a long run would make the figures difficult to read.

  The discussion continues in the next response.
\end{response}

\reviewercomment{
  Differently, it seems that the objective functions of L-BFGS and CN-L-BFGS tend to converge before the iteration cap.
  Does this mean that the positive effect of the initial placement tends to fade faster with L-BFGS, despite the better initial condition?
}

\begin{response}
  The same interpretation applies to this phenomenon. Independently of the merit of the proposed method itself, and simply as a comparison between existing methods, L-BFGS converges faster than the FR algorithm; that is, it approaches an optimal solution more rapidly. Once a method has essentially converged to an optimum, increasing the number of iterations has little effect on the graph layout, because the forces remain nearly balanced. If this optimal state is unique for a given graph, then there is naturally no difference in the final optimum caused by the initial placement. Thus, the effect of the initial placement is more closely related to how quickly the method approaches the optimal state. We revised the manuscript to explain these points clearly.

  We also believe it is important to clarify that the objective-function values plotted in the chart do not perfectly correspond to visual quality. For example, the difference in objective value between a completely random state and a moderately clean layout can be much larger than the difference between a moderately clean layout and a fully clean layout. In continuous optimization, when the optimal objective value \(\Phi(x^*)\) is known, it is common to plot the gap \(\Phi(x) - \Phi(x^*)\) on a logarithmic scale. In our experiments, the optimal objective value is not known, so we simply plot the objective value itself. Therefore, even when the difference in objective value appears sufficiently small---that is, when the difference between RAND-LBFGS and CN-LBFGS appears small---there may still be a sufficiently visible difference in the graph layouts. We added an explanation of these points so that the discussion is more useful and convincing to readers who may have such questions.
\end{response}
